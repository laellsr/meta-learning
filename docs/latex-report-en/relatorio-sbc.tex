\begin{abstract}
Meta-learning systems for algorithm selection train a meta-model to predict the performance of base algorithms (the meta-target) from dataset characteristics (meta-features). While meta-features are routinely scaled, the effect of scaling the meta-target remains largely unexplored. We evaluate four regression meta-models (Ridge, SVR, Random Forest, and MLP) combined with five meta-target scaling strategies across four experimental scenarios, ranging from 50 controlled datasets to 198 real-world OpenML datasets described by either 8 statistical or 78 PyMFE meta-features, and assess both meta-level predictive performance and base-level recommendation quality. Scaling sensitivity depends on meta-model architecture. Without zero-centered standardization, MLP recommendations become statistically indistinguishable from random choice; StandardScaler and RobustScaler reduce its mean absolute error by 51.8\% on controlled data and by up to 36.3\% on real-world data. RobustScaler reduces SVR error by 9.6\% and yields the highest base-level macro-F1 of the study, statistically tying the best static heuristic. Ridge and Random Forest are invariant to linear scalers, whereas the non-linear QuantileTransformer degrades ranking ability for most meta-models. No single scaling technique universally dominates.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

Machine learning (ML) algorithm selection is one of the most resource-intensive stages in building intelligent systems. Rather than exhaustively evaluating every candidate algorithm on a new dataset---a computationally prohibitive approach---\textit{meta-learning} uses historical evaluations to train a meta-model that maps dataset characteristics (\textit{meta-features}) to base algorithm performances (\textit{meta-target}), enabling the recommendation of the most suitable algorithm for unseen tasks~\cite{vanschoren2018meta}.

While input \textit{meta-features} typically undergo rigorous preprocessing and scaling, the performance scores forming the \textit{meta-target} are typically used in their raw form. Nonetheless, metrics such as macro-F1 vary in scale and distribution across tasks, which can hinder meta-models sensitive to these discrepancies. The study by \cite{amorim2022choice} demonstrated at the base level that \textit{``the choice of scaling technique matters for classification performance''}---we investigate whether this effect holds for regression at the meta-level when the targets are scaled.

\textbf{Problem Formulation:} Does the scaling of the meta-target in meta-learning systems for algorithm selection yield meaningful performance differences? Is there a universal scaling recommendation suitable for any meta-model architecture?

\textbf{Hypotheses:} (H1) Meta-models sensitive to target scaling will display statistically significant performance variations across techniques. (H2) Tree-based meta-models will remain invariant to linear target transformations. (H3) No single target scaling technique will universally dominate across all meta-models.

\textbf{Main Contributions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The first systematic investigation into the impact of \textit{meta-target} scaling (as opposed to meta-feature scaling) in regression-based meta-learning systems for algorithm selection;
  \item A dual-level evaluation framework that connects meta-level predictive performance and ranking fidelity to base-level practical utility, measuring end-user macro-F1 gains and regret against an Ideal Oracle and static heuristics;
  \item A progressive empirical benchmark across four experimental scenarios---spanning controlled synthetic tasks and up to 198 real-world OpenML datasets under both basic statistical and expanded PyMFE meta-feature representations---evaluating four meta-model families across five target scaling strategies;
  \item A mechanistic analysis and actionable design guidelines explaining how meta-model architectural properties interact with linear and non-linear target scaling.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Main Findings:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item MLP collapses without zero-centered meta-target standardization: on real-world data its recommendations become statistically indistinguishable from random choice, even though the input meta-features $X$ are standardized---a failure resolved by StandardScaler or RobustScaler;
  \item At the base level, RobustScaler lifts SVR from a losing trend against the Single Best heuristic to a statistical tie, yielding the highest average macro-F1 ($0.7395$) and the lowest regret ($0.0184$) of the study;
  \item Ridge and Random Forest are invariant to linear target scalers, whereas the non-linear QuantileTransformer degrades ranking ability for most meta-models, with MLP under the richer PyMFE meta-feature space as the only exception;
  \item Richer meta-feature sets reduce prediction error across all meta-models when combined with appropriate target scaling.
\end{itemize}

\section{Proposed Approach}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Let $\mathcal{D} = \{d_1, \dots, d_N\}$ denote a benchmark repository of $N$ classification datasets. For each dataset $d_i$, we extract a $D$-dimensional meta-feature vector $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^D$, forming the meta-feature matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$. We evaluate a portfolio of $M$ base classification algorithms $\mathcal{A} = \{a_1, \dots, a_M\}$ on each dataset, yielding a performance vector $\mathbf{y}_i \in \mathbb{R}^M$ (e.g., the macro-F1 score of each algorithm $a_j$ on $d_i$) and the resulting meta-target matrix $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times M}$. The objective is to induce a mapping $f$ that predicts $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i \approx \mathbf{y}_i$ given $\mathbf{x}_i$, subsequently recommending the base algorithm with the highest estimated performance for an unseen dataset.

\subsection{Meta-Learning Pipeline with Meta-Target Scaling}

\subsection{Meta-Target Scaling Techniques}

Five target scaling strategies applied column-wise to the meta-target matrix $Y$ represent distinct scaling paradigms:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Baseline} (Raw data): $y_s = y$. Unscaled control group retaining the original performance values (macro-F1);
  \item \textbf{StandardScaler} (\textbf{Z-score Standardization}): $y_s = (y - \mu) / \sigma$. Centers data around zero mean ($\mu=0$) with unit variance ($\sigma=1$), aiding gradient descent stability;
  \item \textbf{MinMaxScaler} (\textbf{Min-Max Normalization}): $y_s = (y - y_{\min}) / (y_{\max} - y_{\min})$. Linearly bounds values to the closed interval $[0, 1]$, preserving the original distributional shape and relative distances, but retaining a positive offset mean;
  \item \textbf{RobustScaler} (\textbf{Robust Standardization}): $y_s = (y - \text{median}) / \text{IQR}$. Employs outlier-robust statistics (zero median and scaling via the interquartile range $Q_3 - Q_1$), preventing extreme performance values from skewing the distribution;
  \item \textbf{QuantileTransformer} (\textbf{Quantile Transformation}): $y_s = \Phi^{-1}\!\left( \frac{\text{rank}(y)}{n+1} \right)$. Maps data non-linearly to a standard normal distribution via the empirical quantile function, mitigating extreme skewness but distorting relative inter-rank distances.
\end{enumerate}

A key distinction separates these families: StandardScaler and RobustScaler perform \textbf{standardization} (centering with zero mean or median followed by dispersion scaling), a property crucial for stabilizing gradient backpropagation and preventing neuron saturation in neural networks~\cite{lecun2012efficient,glorot2010understanding}. In contrast, MinMaxScaler is a \textbf{range normalization} that compresses scores into $[0, 1]$ without removing the non-zero bias offset (the mean remains around $0.65$ in our benchmark). Techniques 1--4 are strictly \textbf{monotonic} (preserving relative order), whereas quantile transformation is \textbf{non-linear} and may alter the ranking hierarchy among algorithms.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Pipeline evaluation is conducted across two complementary dimensions: the \textbf{meta-level} (meta-model predictive performance and ranking fidelity) and the \textbf{base-level} (practical utility and empirical performance delivered to the end-user).

\subsubsection{Meta-Level Metrics}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{MAE}: Mean Absolute Error evaluated on the original macro-F1 scale;
  \item \textbf{Spearman} ($\rho_s$): Rank correlation between predicted and true rankings of the $M$ base algorithms (ranging in $[-1, +1]$);
  \item \textbf{Acc@1}: Top-1 accuracy, representing the fraction of datasets where the top-predicted algorithm matches the true empirical winner.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Base-Level Metrics}
To measure the concrete utility perceived by a practitioner, let $\mathbf{y}_i \in \mathbb{R}^M$ denote the true macro-F1 vector across the $M$ base algorithms on test dataset $i$, and let $j^*(i) = \arg\max_{j \in \{1, \dots, M\}} \hat{y}_{i, j}$ be the index of the base algorithm recommended at the top of the ranking by the meta-model. We evaluate:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Average Base-Level F1 ($\overline{P}_{\text{base}}$):} The expected performance achieved by following the meta-model's recommendation:
  \begin{equation}
    \overline{P}_{\text{base}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N y_{i, j^*(i)}
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Ideal Oracle ($\overline{P}_{\text{oracle}}$):} The theoretical performance upper bound obtained by perfectly selecting the optimal classifier for each dataset:
  \begin{equation}
    \overline{P}_{\text{oracle}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \max_{j} y_{i, j}
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Average Regret ($\overline{\text{Regret}}$):} The expected performance penalty incurred due to suboptimal recommendations:
  \begin{equation}
    \overline{\text{Regret}} = \overline{P}_{\text{oracle}} - \overline{P}_{\text{base}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left( \max_{j} y_{i, j} - y_{i, j^*(i)} \right)
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Gain over Static Baselines:} We benchmark $\overline{P}_{\text{base}}$ against two static reference strategies requiring no meta-learning: \textit{Single Best} (consistently choosing the classifier with the highest historical training mean, Random Forest) and \textit{Random Choice}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Methodology}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Datasets}

Four distinct experimental setups were conducted:

\textbf{Experiment 1 (controlled):} 50 classification datasets --- 4 real-world benchmarks from \texttt{scikit-learn} (Iris, Wine, Breast Cancer, Digits) and 46 synthetic benchmarks generated via \texttt{make\_classification}, systematically varying instances $N \in [200, 1000]$, features $D \in [6, 25]$, classes $C \in \{2, 3\}$, and Imbalance Ratio $\in [1.0, 20.0]$.

\textbf{Experiment 2 (initial real benchmark):} 144 real-world datasets from OpenML~\cite{vanschoren2014openml}, selected according to curated quality criteria (no missing values, active status, established reference benchmarks).

\textbf{Experiment 3 (expanded real-world):} 200 real-world OpenML datasets retrieved across diverse domain families, ensuring distributional variety and preventing task redundancy by filtering datasets based on name prefixes. Two of them (\texttt{hungarian\_858} and \texttt{Titanic\_40945}) were discarded due to incomplete meta-target evaluations, consolidating the experimental benchmark at 198 datasets. The meta-feature profile is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:metadataset_profile}.

\textbf{Experiment 4 (PyMFE features):} The same datasets from Experiment~3, characterized by 78 meta-features extracted using the \texttt{PyMFE} framework~\cite{pymfe2020} (\texttt{general}, \texttt{statistical}, \texttt{info-theory}, and \texttt{clustering} families), expanding the initial 8 metrics, with median imputation and clipping extreme values exceeding $10^{10}$.

\subsection{Extracted Meta-Features}

Following standard meta-learning practice~\cite{pymfe2020}, we extract tabular meta-features from each dataset. In Experiments 1--3, the representation comprises 8 general and statistical meta-features (from the \texttt{general} and \texttt{statistical} groups), namely: number of instances, number of features, $D/N$ ratio, Imbalance Ratio, Shannon class entropy, mean absolute Pearson correlation between features, mean feature variance, and mean mutual information between features and target classes.

In Experiment~4, this representation space is extended to 78 descriptors via the \texttt{PyMFE} framework, which systematically extracts four feature families ---\texttt{general} (dimensionality, class proportions), \texttt{statistical} (canonical correlation, skewness, variance), \texttt{info-theory} (entropy, mutual information, concentration), and \texttt{clustering} (Calinski-Harabasz, Davies-Bouldin, Dunn)--- subsuming the previous 8 metrics while capturing task complexity.

Across all experiments, input meta-features $X$ were standardized using StandardScaler fitted on the training folds.

\subsection{Base Algorithms and Meta-Target}

Five diverse classification algorithms were evaluated on each dataset via 5-fold stratified cross-validation, using macro-F1 as the performance metric: \textbf{KNN} ($k=5$), \textbf{Decision Tree} (CART), \textbf{Random Forest} (100 estimators), \textbf{Gaussian Naive Bayes}, and \textbf{SVM} (RBF kernel). All algorithms were configured with their default hyperparameters in \texttt{scikit-learn} 1.2.2, with random seeds fixed at 42.

\subsection{Meta-Models}

Four distinct meta-models were evaluated as multi-output regressors (predicting the macro-F1 scores of all 5 base algorithms simultaneously):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ridge} ($\alpha = 1.0$): Regularized linear model;
  \item \textbf{SVR} (RBF kernel, $C=1.0$, $\varepsilon=0.1$): Non-linear kernel regression;
  \item \textbf{Random Forest Regressor} (100 estimators): Tree-based ensemble;
  \item \textbf{MLP Regressor} (hidden layers $[16, 8]$, Adam optimizer, \texttt{max\_iter=2000}): Multi-layer perceptron neural network.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Validation Protocol}

We adopted a \textbf{5-fold cross-validation scheme at the meta-level} (partitioning by datasets). Within each split, the target scaler was fitted exclusively on the training datasets, the meta-model was trained on the scaled targets, and test predictions were inverted back to the original scale before computing evaluation metrics, avoiding data leakage.

\subsection{Statistical Tests}

To assess the statistical significance of performance differences across target scaling techniques, we employed:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Friedman Test} (omnibus): Evaluates whether at least one scaling technique significantly differs from the others per meta-model, based on per-dataset MAE vectors ($\alpha = 0.05$);
  \item \textbf{Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test} (post-hoc): Pairwise comparison of each scaling technique against the unscaled Baseline.
\end{itemize}

At the base level, recommendation quality was further assessed through paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests over the per-dataset macro-F1 vectors of the recommended algorithm ($N = 198$). Comparisons were pre-assigned to two families, each corrected independently via the Holm--Bonferroni step-down procedure ($m = 6$): a \textbf{confirmatory} family, comprising within-model contrasts that isolate the effect of meta-target scaling, and an \textbf{exploratory} family, contrasting meta-models against the static heuristics. Zero differences were discarded following the standard Wilcoxon convention, so the effective sample size $n_{\text{eff}}$ counts only datasets on which the two strategies recommended different algorithms. Effect sizes are reported as the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation $r_{rb}$.

\subsection{Results Across Experiments}

This section summarizes the main findings across the four benchmarks.

Experiment 1 (50 controlled datasets) confirmed initial hypotheses: Ridge and Random Forest were unaffected by linear target scalers, SVR displayed moderate improvements in error and ranking, and MLP exhibited partial degradation without target standardization.

Experiments 2 and 3 (144 and 198 real-world datasets characterized by 8 statistical meta-features) corroborated these trends on realistic data. The MLP failure was particularly pronounced: in Experiment 2, Spearman correlation fell to 0.034 (reflecting zero ranking capability) and Acc@1 dropped to 12.5\% (well below the 20\% random baseline for 5 candidate algorithms). Conversely, target standardization via StandardScaler or RobustScaler restored predictive capacity, reducing MAE to the range of 0.11 to 0.13 and raising Spearman correlation to values between 0.41 and 0.45. Table~\ref{tab:mlp_comparison} in Section~\ref{subsec:analise_mlp} summarizes MLP performance across all four experiments.

Experiment 4 (the same 198 datasets characterized by 78 PyMFE meta-features) is the largest-scale experiment in this study. Using richer descriptors reduced prediction errors across all properly calibrated models. For instance, the minimum MAE achieved by SVR dropped from 0.123 (in Experiment 3) to 0.088 (in Experiment 4), while Random Forest MAE fell from 0.114 to 0.085. The base-level evaluation shows several practical patterns:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Target standardization renders SVR practically viable:} Without target scaling (Baseline), SVR achieves a base F1 of $0.7296$, trending below the trivial heuristic of deploying Random Forest uniformly across all tasks ($0.7323$; $p_{\text{nom}} = 0.078$, $r_{rb} = -0.212$). Under \textbf{robust standardization (RobustScaler)}, SVR rises to \textbf{$0.7395$} --- the highest average F1 in the study --- reducing \textit{Average Regret} to $0.0184$ (less than 2 percentage points from the Ideal Oracle). This within-model gain of $+0.0099$ is significant (49 wins against 19 losses, $p_{\text{adj}} = 6.4 \times 10^{-4}$, $r_{rb} = +0.527$), converting a losing trend against the static heuristic into a statistical tie ($p_{\text{adj}} = 0.260$).
  \item \textbf{Practical severity of the MLP collapse:} Unscaled MLP delivers a base F1 of $0.6576$, incurring a regret exceeding $10\%$ against the Oracle ($\text{Regret} = 0.1003$) and losing decisively to the Single Best heuristic (101 losses against 30 wins, $p_{\text{adj}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-13}$, $r_{rb} = -0.772$), while remaining \textbf{statistically indistinguishable from Random Choice} ($p_{\text{adj}} = 0.260$). Zero-mean or zero-median target standardization recovers the model to $0.7062$ (StandardScaler, $+0.0486$, $p_{\text{adj}} = 3.3 \times 10^{-6}$, $r_{rb} = +0.515$) and $0.7028$ (RobustScaler, $+0.0452$, $p_{\text{adj}} = 2.6 \times 10^{-6}$), cutting regret nearly in half and restoring a solid advantage over chance ($p_{\text{adj}} = 1.3 \times 10^{-13}$). MinMaxScaler, in contrast, is inert (175 ties out of 198, $p_{\text{adj}} = 0.471$).
  \item \textbf{Resilience of Random Forest:} The tree ensemble exhibits inherent robustness, maintaining performance around $0.736$--$0.738$ across all target scaling techniques; switching from Baseline to MinMaxScaler leaves the recommendation unchanged on 185 of the 198 datasets ($p_{\text{adj}} = 0.471$). Its best configuration posts the only nominally significant gain over the Single Best heuristic ($+0.0058$, 36 wins against 15 losses, $p_{\text{nom}} = 0.020$, $r_{rb} = +0.374$), but this advantage does not survive multiplicity control within the exploratory family ($p_{\text{adj}} = 0.080$) and should therefore be read as a consistent trend rather than demonstrated superiority.
  \item \textbf{Degradation induced by QuantileTransformer:} Non-linear quantile distortions raised the regret of Ridge from $0.0365$ to $0.0419$ relative to the Oracle. At the base level, however, this degradation amounts only to a trend ($-0.0054$, 43 wins against 54 losses, $p_{\text{nom}} = 0.051$, $p_{\text{adj}} = 0.154$): the evidence that QuantileTransformer harms Ridge is statistically solid on the ranking metrics (Acc@1 and Spearman, Table~\ref{tab:results_exp4_summary}) rather than on base-level F1.
\end{enumerate}

Two aspects of Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon_base} are worth noting. First, the high proportion of ties (126 to 185 out of 198 datasets) indicates that the compared strategies frequently recommend the same base algorithm, so the differences concentrate on the remaining third of the benchmark --- the contested subset isolated by the rank-biserial coefficients. Second, unscaled MLP wins on more datasets than it loses against Random Choice (116 versus 82, $r_{rb} = +0.124$) while still yielding a lower aggregate mean ($\Delta\text{F1} = -0.0092$). This asymmetry reflects large losses on specific tasks, where the network loses by large margins. Under the paired test, this difference is not statistically significant ($W = 8628.0$, $p_{\text{nom}} = 0.130$, $p_{\text{adj}} = 0.260$).

\subsection{Meta-Model Analysis}

\subsubsection{Ridge Regression: Invariance to Linear Transformations}

Ridge regression produced \textit{identical} results under linear transformations (StandardScaler, MinMaxScaler, and RobustScaler) across all four experiments. This invariance follows from the algebraic properties of regularized linear models: under an affine target transformation $y_s = a \cdot y + b$, Ridge analytically rescales its regression coefficients, and post-prediction inversion exactly reconstructs the original output. The sole exception is QuantileTransformer: by applying a non-linear mapping, it distorts relative distances in the target space, so that post-prediction inversion no longer preserves rankings, causing the observed drop in recommendation accuracy (Acc@1).

\subsubsection{SVR: Target Scaling Benefits Recommendation More than Error Reduction}

For SVR, target scaling exerts a substantial impact on recommendation quality (Acc@1 and Spearman correlation) across all benchmarks, even when the reduction in absolute error (MAE) is modest. On real-world datasets characterized by basic meta-features (Experiments 2 and 3), absolute error remained largely unchanged, yet the ability to identify the top-performing classifier improved markedly: in Experiment 2, Acc@1 rose from 0.486 to 0.563 with RobustScaler, and in Experiment 3 from 0.429 to 0.470. Under the rich PyMFE meta-feature space of Experiment 4, SVR improved across all metrics: MAE dropped by approximately 10\% (from 0.098 to 0.088 with RobustScaler) while recommendation accuracy increased from 0.369 to 0.495 under StandardScaler.

\subsubsection{Random Forest: Robustness to Meta-Target Scaling}

Random Forest proved to be the most stable meta-model against variations in meta-target distribution. Across all experiments, MAE differences among linear scaling techniques were negligible ($< 0.007$), with statistical tests confirming no global significance. This follows from the split criterion: regression trees select splits by minimizing the residual variance of the target, and variance is invariant --- up to a constant factor that does not alter the arg\,min --- under any affine transformation $y \mapsto a \cdot y + b$. StandardScaler, MinMaxScaler and RobustScaler are therefore unable to alter the induced tree structure. Variance is not preserved, however, under non-linear mappings, even monotonic ones: QuantileTransformer reshapes the target density and consequently the relative distances between performance scores, explaining why it is the only technique that degrades Random Forest recommendation accuracy (Acc@1). At the base level, replacing the Baseline with MinMaxScaler leaves the recommended algorithm unchanged on 185 of the 198 datasets (93.4\%), leaving only $n_{\text{eff}} = 13$ contested decisions --- a sample on which the paired test has virtually no power, so the invariance claim rests on the tie count rather than on the resulting $p$-value ($p_{\text{adj}} = 0.471$).

\subsubsection{MLP: Sensitivity to Meta-Target Standardization}
\label{subsec:analise_mlp}

MLP exhibited the strongest sensitivity to target scaling. Without meta-target standardization (i.e., when trained on raw or Min-Max normalized targets), the network fails to learn useful ranking patterns from real-world data. Spearman correlation drops to near-zero levels (0.034 in Exp. 2, 0.167 in Exp. 3, and 0.080 in Exp. 4), recommendation accuracy hovers near or below the 20\% random baseline (reaching 12.5\% in Exp. 2), and base-level performance drops to $0.658$ (statistically indistinguishable from random choice, $p_{\text{adj}} = 0.260$). This breakdown occurs on real-world benchmarks because base algorithm macro-F1 scores share a consistently high positive mean ($\approx 0.65$ to $0.75$), coupled with high variance across datasets. When the meta-target lacks zero-centered standardization ($\mu=0$ or zero median), standard neural network weight initialization (which presumes zero-centered outputs)~\cite{glorot2010understanding,lecun2012efficient} causes the MSE loss to be dominated by fitting this constant bias offset, reducing gradient sensitivity to the relative differences among the 5 base algorithms. Zero-centering methods like StandardScaler and RobustScaler eliminate this offset, allowing backpropagation to calibrate weights based on relative performance differentials.

MinMaxScaler fails to resolve this instability because it performs a linear contraction to $[0, 1]$, preserving the non-zero positive offset and skewness of the original distribution (explaining why its results remain statistically indistinguishable from the Baseline). Conversely, in Experiment 4 (with 78 PyMFE descriptors), a distinct behavior emerges: QuantileTransformer delivered the highest Spearman correlation for MLP (0.436). This shows that in dense, structured meta-feature spaces, mapping the target to a Gaussian distribution helps the neural network capture hierarchical relationships among base classifiers.

\subsection{The QuantileTransformer Trade-off: Rank Uniformity vs.\ Density Distortions}

While QuantileTransformer may reduce absolute error (MAE) by projecting targets into a standard normal distribution, it generally harms algorithm recommendation accuracy (Acc@1). This distortion occurs because non-linear quantile mapping warps the relative distances between algorithm performance scores. For example, if two classifiers achieve close scores (e.g., 0.70 and 0.71), quantile transformation may push their mapped values substantially apart, whereas distant performances (e.g., 0.50 and 0.70) may be compressed. This non-linear distortion hinders post-prediction inversion from preserving the true ranking hierarchy.

Consequently, whenever the primary goal is \textbf{optimal algorithm recommendation}, QuantileTransformer should be avoided for most meta-models due to its ranking degradation. In principle, quantile transformation is designed to map highly skewed or heavy-tailed continuous distributions into a Gaussian shape; however, for algorithm recommendation, its non-linear mapping alters relative performance distances, breaking rank preservation. The sole exception to this ranking degradation occurred with MLP in Experiment 4 (with PyMFE), where richer meta-features helped compensate for non-linear mapping distortions.

\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================

This study investigated how meta-target scaling techniques influence the performance of meta-learning systems for algorithm selection. Four progressive experimental setups were evaluated: 50 controlled benchmark datasets, 144 and 198 real-world OpenML datasets characterized by 8 statistical meta-features, and the same 198 datasets expanded to 78 descriptors via the PyMFE framework.

Our findings show that \textbf{meta-target scaling sensitivity depends on meta-model architecture} and affects the practical utility of recommendations at the base level:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ridge}: Remains invariant to linear target scaling due to its mathematical formulation, but suffers ranking degradation and elevated base-level regret under non-linear transformations.
  \item \textbf{SVR}: Substantially improves recommendation quality (Acc@1 and Spearman correlation) under standardization (StandardScaler or RobustScaler). In base-level evaluation, SVR coupled with RobustScaler achieves the highest average performance ($F1 = 0.7395$) and the lowest regret ($0.0184$) in the entire study. Target standardization is what makes this possible: the within-model gain over unscaled SVR is highly significant ($p_{\text{adj}} < 10^{-3}$, $r_{rb} = +0.527$), lifting the model from a losing trend against the best static heuristic to a statistical tie with it.
  \item \textbf{Random Forest}: The most stable meta-model overall, maintaining high base-level performance ($F1 \approx 0.738$) invariant to target scaling; its performance remains statistically identical across all linear scalers.
  \item \textbf{MLP}: Zero-mean or zero-median target standardization (StandardScaler or RobustScaler) is required. Without it, the network exhibits severe base-level degradation, yielding $F1 = 0.658$ --- statistically indistinguishable from random choice and worse than the Single Best heuristic ($p_{\text{adj}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-13}$).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{QuantileTransformer should generally be avoided when the primary goal is algorithm recommendation}, as its non-linear mapping distorts ranking hierarchies for most traditional meta-models; its harm is statistically established on ranking metrics, though dense meta-feature spaces allow neural architectures (MLP) to recover ranking correlations.

As a complementary methodological contribution, the transition from the initial 8 meta-features to the 78 PyMFE descriptors not only reduced absolute prediction error across all properly calibrated meta-models, but also lowered residual noise enough to render the effect of meta-target scaling detectable by the Friedman test for Ridge and SVR --- sensitivities that remained masked under the reduced feature set.

These results allow us to address each hypothesis:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1 is confirmed} for the architectures it targets: meta-models relying on gradient descent (MLP) or on margins and distances in the target space (SVR) exhibited statistically significant sensitivity to meta-target scaling ($p = 5.9 \times 10^{-59}$ and $p = 5.5 \times 10^{-11}$ in Experiment 4). Ridge, having a closed-form solution, is invariant to affine target transformations; its significance in Experiment 4 stems from the non-linearity of QuantileTransformer.
  \item \textbf{H2 is confirmed}: the tree ensemble remained insensitive to every linear target transformation, recommending the identical algorithm on 93.4\% of the datasets, and degraded only under the non-linear mapping of QuantileTransformer.
  \item \textbf{H3 is confirmed}: no single technique dominates universally, and the optimal choice depends on meta-model architecture.
\end{itemize}

Accordingly, when the meta-model family is already fixed, the designer should adopt the technique empirically demonstrated for that architecture: RobustScaler for SVR, any linear scaler (or raw target) for Ridge and Random Forest, and StandardScaler or RobustScaler for MLP. As a fallback guideline for settings in which several architectures will be explored without budget for a scaling search, \textbf{RobustScaler} stands out as the most conservative option --- not because it maximizes expected F1 across all models, but because it minimizes risk: it prevents the catastrophic collapse of the neural network (unlike MinMaxScaler and the Baseline), renders SVR competitive, and preserves the invariance of Random Forest and Ridge.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

The scope of these conclusions is bounded by four design decisions. First, the five base classifiers were run with \texttt{scikit-learn} default hyperparameters, without AutoML or hyperparameter optimization; the meta-target therefore characterizes a portfolio of untuned algorithms. Second, the meta-target is restricted to macro-F1, leaving aside probability calibration metrics (Brier score, log-loss) and runtime cost; bounded or asymmetric metrics may alter the observed scaling interactions, warranting dedicated investigation. Third, datasets were capped at $2{,}000$ instances to keep 5-fold cross-validation over 198 problems computationally feasible, which limits extrapolation to large-scale tasks. Fourth, the MLP was evaluated with a fixed $[16, 8]$ architecture, so the observed collapse may be amplified by \texttt{scikit-learn} defaults --- Glorot/He initialization presuming zero-centered outputs, Adam with a fixed learning rate and no warm-up, and the absence of batch normalization. Future work should assess whether modern deep learning practices (layer normalization, output-bias initialization, or ranking-oriented losses such as ListNet) mitigate this intrinsic sensitivity, and whether these findings hold for other meta-targets and larger dataset regimes.
